### 1. WHAT IS IT?

The sampling rate `sr` (in Hz) is how many amplitude measurements are taken per second when digitizing sound. The Nyquist theorem says a signal can be faithfully reconstructed only if it contains no frequency above `sr / 2` (the Nyquist frequency).

### 2. WHY DOES IT EXIST?

A continuous signal has infinitely many values; computers need finite data. The theorem tells us the cheapest rate that loses nothing (for band-limited signals), and tells us what goes wrong below it: aliasing.

### 3. DETAILED EXPLANATION

**Nyquist.** To capture frequency `f` you need more than two samples per cycle: `sr > 2 f`. Human hearing reaches roughly 20 kHz, hence CD audio at 44.1 kHz; telephone speech is 8 kHz (content up to about 3.4 kHz); speech models usually use 16 kHz (content to 8 kHz) (`REVIEW_REQUIRED` for standards details).

**Aliasing.** A frequency above `sr / 2` folds back: its apparent frequency is `|f - k*sr|` for the nearest integer `k`; in code `alias_frequency(f, sr)`. A 5 kHz tone sampled at 8 kHz *looks exactly like* a 3 kHz tone and no later processing can tell them apart. Real converters therefore apply an analog **anti-aliasing low-pass filter** before sampling, and software resamplers low-pass before decimating.

**Resampling.** Converting 44.1 kHz audio to 16 kHz for a speech model means: low-pass at 8 kHz, then interpolate. Skipping the low-pass folds energy above 8 kHz into the speech band. The lab's `resample_linear(..., lowpass=True)` shows this; a test asserts that a 6 kHz tone vanishes after low-pass resampling to 8 kHz but survives as an alias without it.

**Quantization and bit depth.** Each sample is rounded to one of `2^b` levels; for a full-scale sine the SNR is about `6.02 b + 1.76` dB, i.e. roughly 6 dB per bit. 16 bits is about 98 dB; 8 bits about 50 dB. (The lab test checks the ~24 dB difference between 8 and 12 bits.)

**Why 16 kHz for ASR.** It keeps most phonetic information, halves cost versus 32 kHz, and is what most pretrained models expect. Feeding the wrong rate shifts every frequency: a model trained on 16 kHz and given 8 kHz audio labelled 16 kHz sees voices at double pitch and speed.

### 5. SIMPLE EXAMPLE

A film camera shooting a spinning wheel at 24 frames/s: when the wheel spins fast the spokes seem to turn slowly or backwards. That illusion is aliasing.

### 6. TECHNICAL EXAMPLE

A voice assistant records 48 kHz microphone audio, downsamples to 16 kHz for ASR (low-pass first), and keeps 24 kHz for the TTS reply. Using the wrong rate on a Whisper-style model silently degrades word error rate (`REVIEW_REQUIRED` for specific numbers).

### 7. CODE

```python
# run inside lab/
from audio_core import tone, alias_frequency, dominant_freq, resample_linear, quantize, snr_db

print(alias_frequency(5000, 8000))                 # 3000.0: folds below Nyquist (4000)
x = tone(5000, 1.0, 8000)
print(round(dominant_freq(x, 8000)))               # 3000: measured, matches the formula

hi = tone(6000, 1.0, 16000)                        # 6 kHz speech-band killer
good = resample_linear(hi, 16000, 8000, lowpass=True)
bad = resample_linear(hi, 16000, 8000, lowpass=False)
print(abs(good).max() < 0.1, abs(bad).max() > 0.5)  # True True

x = tone(220, 1.0, 16000, amp=0.99)
print(round(snr_db(x, quantize(x, 8))), round(snr_db(x, quantize(x, 12))))  # ~50 ~74
```

### 9. ENTERPRISE USE CASE

Contact-centre analytics ingests 8 kHz phone recordings and 16 kHz app recordings; a data-validation step records the sample rate per file and routes narrow-band audio to a model trained or fine-tuned for it.

### 10. AI ENGINEERING CONNECTION

Every pretrained speech model fixes an expected rate; mismatch is a silent accuracy bug. Neural codecs ([14.8](14.08-audio-tokenization-and-neural-codecs.md)) and vocoders ([14.17](14.17-vocoders.md)) also fix sample rates (for example 24 kHz for many TTS vocoders, `REVIEW_REQUIRED`).

### 11. ADVANTAGES

Clear theory (Nyquist), small and predictable data volumes, easy to validate in code.

### 12. LIMITATIONS

Lower rates lose high-frequency detail (fricatives like 's' lose energy above 4 kHz); resampling is lossy and cannot recover removed content.

### 13. TRADE-OFFS

Higher rate: better fidelity, more data, more compute. Lower rate: cheap but narrow-band (telephone) quality.

### 14. COMMON MISTAKES

- Resampling without a low-pass filter.
- Ignoring the file header's sample rate and assuming 16 kHz.
- Resampling repeatedly (accumulating error).
- Training and serving with different resamplers.

### 15. PERFORMANCE / COST CONSIDERATIONS

Compute and memory scale linearly with `sr`: 48 kHz is 3x the samples of 16 kHz. Resample once at ingest and cache.

### 16. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Out-of-band content is a known attack surface: inaudible ultrasonic commands have been injected into voice assistants (the 'DolphinAttack' class, `REVIEW_REQUIRED` for specifics). Band-limiting at ingest and anomaly checks on spectra are partial mitigations; never assume 'inaudible' means 'absent'.

### 17. INTERVIEW EXPLANATION

*"Sampling rate is samples per second. Nyquist says you need more than twice the highest frequency you care about, otherwise higher frequencies fold down as aliases you cannot undo. So we low-pass before downsampling, and for speech models we standardise on 16 kHz. The trade-off is fidelity versus data and compute."*

### 18. RAPID-FIRE ANSWER

Samples per second; must exceed twice the top frequency or aliasing folds it into lower frequencies.
