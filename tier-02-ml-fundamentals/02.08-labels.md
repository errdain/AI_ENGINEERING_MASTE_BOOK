# 2.8 Labels

**Metadata** — Tier: 2 · Phase: 1 · Priority: P0 · Difficulty: 1/5 · Interview: High · Hands-on: High
Prerequisites: 2.2, 2.7 · Leads to: 2.10 Dataset construction, 2.37–2.43 Metrics, Tier 15 Evaluation

### 1. What is it?
The **target values** the model is trained to predict: classes, numbers, spans, rankings, preferences. The `y` in `(x, y)`.

### 2. Why does it exist?
Supervised learning is only as good as its labels: the model optimizes agreement with whatever the labels say, right or wrong.

### 3. Detailed explanation
- **Sources:** human annotation, logged outcomes (clicked, defaulted), rules/heuristics (weak supervision), model-generated labels (pseudo-labels, LLM-as-annotator).
- **Proxy labels** measure something correlated with the real goal (clicks for satisfaction). Optimizing the proxy can diverge from the goal.
- **Label noise hurts:** with 40% of training labels flipped at random, held-out accuracy on a simple task drops from 92.1% to 72.7% (average over 10 seeds). Noise in the *test* labels also caps measurable accuracy.
- **Delayed labels:** outcomes (fraud, churn) arrive weeks later; training data is therefore stale and must handle right-censoring.
- **Quality controls:** annotation guidelines, multiple annotators, agreement measures, gold sets, audits, versioned label definitions.
- **Class balance** of labels drives metric choice (2.45, 2.51).
- **LLM-generated labels** are cheap but share the model's biases; validate on a human-labeled sample.

### 4. How does it work?
Define the label precisely → collect → check quality → freeze a versioned definition → split without leakage (2.10).

### 5. Simple example
The answer key. If the key has mistakes, students learn the mistakes.

### 6. Technical example
Sentiment classification: each review labeled positive/negative by three annotators with majority vote; disagreement items are reviewed.

### 8. Input → Process → Output
`raw outcome / annotation → label definition → label column y`

### 9. Enterprise use case
Labeling programs need budgets, tooling, guidelines, QA and versioning; disagreement analysis often reveals ambiguous product requirements.

### 10. AI engineering connection
Preference labels for alignment (Tier 10); evaluation sets with gold answers (Tier 30); LLM judges (use with calibration).

### 11. Advantages
Provide the objective signal; improve directly with data investment.

### 12. Limitations
Subjective, expensive, biased, delayed; define the problem as much as they measure it.

### 13. Trade-offs
Cheap noisy labels at scale vs expensive clean labels in small amounts; often mix both.

### 14. Common mistakes
Vague definitions; using the label to build features; mixing label versions; ignoring annotator disagreement; training and testing on the same noisy source.

### 15. Performance / cost
Annotation throughput is the usual bottleneck; active learning (label the uncertain cases) reduces it.

### 16. Security
Annotators may see sensitive data; manage access, consent and anonymization. Adversarial labelers can poison data.

### 17. Interview explanation
"Labels are the targets the model learns. Their quality bounds model quality: I define them precisely, measure annotator agreement, and watch for proxy labels that drift from the real goal. Label noise hurts both training and the reliability of evaluation."

### 18. Rapid-fire
Labels = targets y; quality caps performance; watch noise, proxies and delay.
