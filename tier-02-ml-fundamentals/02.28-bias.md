# 2.28 Bias

**Metadata** — Tier: 2 · Phase: 1 · Priority: P0 · Difficulty: 2/5 · Interview: High · Hands-on: Medium

### 1. What is it?
In this concept, **model bias** (statistical bias): the systematic difference between the average prediction of a learning procedure and the true function, i.e. error that remains even with unlimited training sets.

### 2. Why does it exist?
Bias explains underfitting and tells you whether a simpler/richer model class can ever fit the truth.

### 3. Detailed explanation
**The word 'bias' has five different meanings in this book. Keep them apart:**
1. **Model/statistical bias (this concept):** `Bias(x) = E_D[f̂(x)] − f(x)`, averaged over training sets `D`. Squared and averaged over inputs it is the `bias²` of 2.30.
2. **Inductive bias:** assumptions built into a model or learner (linearity, locality in CNNs, attention patterns) that let it generalize from limited data.
3. **Dataset / sampling bias:** the training data does not represent the target population (2.9, 2.10).
4. **Societal / fairness bias:** unjust disparities in outcomes across groups (Tier 32).
5. **The bias term** `b` in `w·x + b` (3.7). Unrelated to the others despite the name.

- **Demonstration (true `sin(πx)`, noise σ=0.2):** a degree-1 model has `bias² = 0.205` (far above the 0.04 noise floor) however many datasets you average; a degree-5 model has `bias² = 4×10⁻⁵`. Because sine is an odd function, degree 2 adds no bias reduction over degree 1 (both 0.205), and degree 4 over degree 3 (both 0.005).
- **Sampling bias demonstration:** a degree-5 polynomial trained only on `x ∈ [−1, 0]` has MSE 0.0018 inside that range and **48.3** on `x ∈ [0, 1]`: the training distribution defines where the model can be trusted.
- **Reducing model bias:** richer model class, better features, less regularization, ensembling of diverse models.
- **Bias and fairness are different problems:** a model can have low statistical bias and still produce disparate outcomes (if the labels or data reflect past discrimination), and vice versa.

### 4. How does it work?
Imagine training the same procedure on many datasets; the gap between the average prediction and the truth is the bias. In practice it is estimated by simulation, or inferred from high training error.

### 5. Simple example
A scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy: more weighings don't remove the error.

### 6. Technical example
Linear model on a quadratic relationship: systematic under-prediction in the middle and over-prediction at the ends.

### 8. Input → Process → Output
`restricted model class → systematic error independent of sample size`

### 9. Enterprise use case
Audits separate 'model too simple' (statistical bias) from 'data unrepresentative' (sampling bias) and 'unfair outcomes' (governance).

### 10. AI engineering connection
Inductive biases of architectures (attention, convolution) and training-data bias in LLMs shape behaviour; evaluate across slices (Tier 30, 32).

### 11. Advantages
(Of knowing it) tells you exactly when more data is useless.

### 12. Limitations
True function is unknown; bias is estimated, not observed directly.

### 13. Trade-offs
Lower bias via flexibility raises variance (2.29, 2.30).

### 14. Common mistakes
Using 'bias' without saying which one; assuming more data reduces model bias; ignoring sampling bias because overall accuracy is high.

### 15. Performance / cost
Reducing bias often needs larger models or features: higher compute.

### 16. Security
Dataset and societal bias create legal/ethical exposure; document data provenance and test on protected-group slices.

### 17. Interview explanation
"Bias in the statistical sense is the systematic gap between the average model prediction and the truth — it's what causes underfitting and doesn't shrink with more data. I distinguish it from dataset bias, fairness bias, inductive bias, and the intercept term, because people use the word for all five."

### 18. Rapid-fire
Bias = systematic error of the model class (underfitting); distinct from dataset, fairness and inductive bias.
