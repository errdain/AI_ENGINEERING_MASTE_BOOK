### 1. WHAT IS IT?

The empirical finding that a language model's test loss falls as a **smooth power law** in model size N, training tokens D and compute C (Kaplan et al. 2020), so loss is *predictable*: train small models, fit a curve, forecast a large run. The Chinchilla parametric form (Hoffmann et al. 2022) is

`L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β`

### 2. WHY DOES IT EXIST?

Pretraining runs are too expensive to tune by trial and error. Scaling laws let you choose N and D before launch, compare architectures and mixtures at small scale, and detect a misbehaving run (above the predicted curve).

### 3. DETAILED EXPLANATION

`E` is the irreducible loss (entropy of the text under this setup); the other two terms are the penalties for finite model size and finite data. Minimising `L` subject to `6ND = C` (Lagrange multipliers; implemented as `scaling.compute_optimal` and verified as a minimum by `test_compute_optimal_respects_budget_and_is_a_minimum`) gives

`N_opt ∝ C^{β/(α+β)}`, `D_opt ∝ C^{α/(α+β)}`: **scale parameters and tokens together.**

**Constants.** `scaling.py` uses `E=1.69, A=406.4, B=410.7, α=0.34, β=0.28`, the values I recall from that paper's parametric fit. **`REVIEW_REQUIRED`: verify against the paper; they describe *that* paper's data, tokenizer and architecture and will not transfer to yours.** Replication work has questioned details of that fit (`REVIEW_REQUIRED`), so use the *shape* of the argument, not the digits. With these constants the optimal tokens-per-parameter ratio **grows with compute** (about 26 at 1e18 FLOPs, 40 at 1e20, 98 at 1e24 in this code), whereas the popular "about 20 tokens per parameter" comes from the paper's other estimation approaches, so do not quote one magic ratio.

### 4. HOW DOES IT WORK?

1. Train a ladder of small models (several sizes, with matched token budgets and tokenizer). 2. Record final validation loss. 3. Fit `L = E + A/N^α (+ B/D^β)` (the lab's `fit_power_law` grid-searches `E`, then least squares in log space). 4. Extrapolate within a sensible range. 5. Choose N and D for your compute by the optimum. 6. Monitor the big run against the predicted curve.

### 5. SIMPLE EXAMPLE

A student's marks improve smoothly with study hours and with the number of practice problems; with a fixed total time you split between reading and practising, and the best split is neither extreme.

### 6. TECHNICAL EXAMPLE

For `C = 1e22` FLOPs the lab's optimum gives a model of about 5B parameters trained on about 320B tokens (see `compute_optimal`); doubling parameters while halving tokens at the same `C` gives a *higher* predicted loss.

### 7. CODE

```python
# Run from lab/: compute-optimal split, loss prediction, and fitting a power law to synthetic data.
import numpy as np
from scaling import compute_optimal, loss, fit_power_law

C = 1e22
N, D = compute_optimal(C)
print(f"N*={N:.3e}  D*={D:.3e}  D/N={D/N:.1f}  predicted loss={loss(N, D):.3f}")
print("check 6ND == C:", abs(6 * N * D - C) / C < 1e-9)

Ns = np.array([1e6, 3e6, 1e7, 3e7, 1e8])
Ls = 1.0 + 20.0 / Ns ** 0.3                              # synthetic "measurements"
print(fit_power_law(Ns, Ls))                             # recovers E near 1.0 and alpha near 0.3
```

### 8. INPUT → PROCESS → OUTPUT

```
small-run losses (N, D, L) -> fit L(N,D) -> extrapolate -> choose (N*, D*) for compute C -> compare big run to curve
```

### 9. ENTERPRISE USE CASE

Before approving a custom-pretraining budget, run a small ladder on your own data to predict the loss of the target model size, and re-fit when the data or tokenizer changes.

### 10. AI ENGINEERING CONNECTION

Scaling laws explain why labs release families of sizes, why small models over-trained on more tokens are attractive for serving, and why model cards cite parameters *and* tokens. They also frame the "data wall" debate.

### 11. ADVANTAGES

- Predictable, cheap decisions before an expensive run.
- Framework for comparing data and architecture changes at small scale.

### 12. LIMITATIONS

- Describes loss on the training distribution, not capability or downstream benchmark scores.
- Fits within a range; breaks down with heavy repetition, quality shifts, and architectural changes.
- Constants are specific to the setup that produced them.

### 13. TRADE-OFFS

Compute-optimal training minimises *training* cost for a loss; over-training a smaller model costs more training compute but less inference ([9.21](09.21-compute-data-model-scaling-trade-offs.md)).

### 14. COMMON MISTAKES

- Extrapolating far beyond the fitted range.
- Mixing tokenizers across points.
- Fitting with too few points or ignoring seed noise.
- Treating the recalled constants as universal.

### 15. PERFORMANCE / COST CONSIDERATIONS

A scaling ladder costs a small fraction (often a few percent; `REVIEW_REQUIRED`) of the final run, but saves you from mis-sizing it. Use matched learning-rate tuning per size or the fit is biased.

### 16. SECURITY CONSIDERATIONS

Scaling predictions can leak planned model sizes and budgets; treat internal fits as confidential. Larger models memorise more, so privacy risk grows with N.

### 17. INTERVIEW EXPLANATION

"Scaling laws say loss falls as a power law in parameters and in tokens, so for a fixed compute budget there's a best split. Chinchilla showed many earlier models were too big and under-trained: scale N and D together, roughly tens of tokens per parameter. I'd still fit my own curve, and if the model will be served at huge volume I'd deliberately over-train a smaller model, because inference cost depends on N."

### 18. RAPID-FIRE ANSWER

`L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β`; for fixed compute, grow model and data together.
